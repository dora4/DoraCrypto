## A Chilling News Story

On September 4, 2026, Gangnam Unni, a South Korean medical aesthetics platform, suffered a cyberattack, exposing private data of 220,000 users from home and abroad.

The leaked information included: names, phone numbers, email addresses, dates of birth, social media accounts, access IPs, as well as consultation items, hospital and physician names, reasons for consultation, medical progress, pre-op and post-op photos uploaded by users, appointment times, treatment records, and payment details.

South Korean media pointed out that such data can reveal users’ health conditions and appearance concerns. Attackers may also impersonate the platform or clinics to launch targeted phishing scams under the guise of promotional offers. Worse still, criminals may blackmail victims by threatening to release their pre-surgery photos and treatment records.

220,000 entries. Not just a number — they represent 220,000 real people whose cosmetic treatment secrets, anxieties and pre-operation photos were laid bare on the internet.

## Why Ordinary Storage Is Not Enough

Many people think: “My phone has a lock screen password, so I should be safe enough.”

It is not enough. Here’s why:

**1. Lock screen passwords ≠ data encryption**
Most phone lock screen passwords only control access to the device, not whether the underlying data is encrypted. If attackers bypass the lock screen via physical means (e.g. reading storage chips by disassembling the device) or system vulnerabilities, your files will be exposed directly.

**2. Cloud sync may amplify risks**
When you sync phone photos to the cloud, your ID photos now exist in at least two locations. A breach at either end can trigger data leakage.

**3. “Deletion” does not mean “disappeared”**
When you delete an ID photo in the file manager, only the file index is removed. The raw data remains on the storage chip until overwritten by new data. Professional data recovery tools can retrieve it easily.

**4. Apps may be “snooping”**
One key focus of the 2026 special campaign on personal information protection is cracking down on apps that collect user data beyond permitted scope. You may believe a flashlight app requests album access merely to “select an avatar”, yet it may upload every photo in your gallery.

**Q1: How is it different from the built-in “private safe vault” on my phone?**
Most native phone private vaults use device-level encryption with keys managed by phone manufacturers. If the manufacturer’s key management system is breached (this has happened before), your data may be decrypted. Dora Box puts keys fully under your control, with no reliance on any third party.
