# After 1.52 Million Records Leaked, People Are Searching for Their Names

## — When Your Data Ends Up Where It Should Never Be

On November 22, Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) released an unsettling notice.

It was not a power outage alert or a rate hike announcement — it confirmed yet another massive data breach.

Its customer service platform *Hanjeon ON* and electronic procurement system came under external attack, exposing roughly 1.52 million sets of personal information. Of these, 650,000 records came from regular user accounts and 870,000 from the procurement system. The leaked data included names, residential addresses, contact details and business registration information.

Just one month prior, KEPCO disclosed another leak: records for 35,000 current and retired staff were compromised.

Two breaches in a single month, adding up to more than 1.55 million records.

South Korean media headlines remained restrained, yet comment sections told a different story. Many users were asking: “How can I check whether my information has been leaked?”

No one could give them an answer. The stolen data had flowed onto the dark web — and the dark web sends no breach notifications.

---

## A Fact You May Have Overlooked

When news like this breaks, most people react with a weary sense of resignation: *Another leak.* Over the past decade, data breaches have gone from shocking to normalized.

But let’s look at it from another angle, and a far more thought-provoking question emerges.

KEPCO’s users did not voluntarily place their data on the *Hanjeon ON* platform. KEPCO collected the data, stored it on its own servers — and then hackers stole it.

Your personal information was never truly in your control from start to finish.

This is far from an isolated case. Rakuten Securities in Japan suffered a breach exposing 15.72 million accounts, with data held on Rakuten’s systems. TSMC saw 46,000 employee records leak from a vendor’s server. Mitsubishi UFJ Financial Group had 8 million customer details compromised on servers operated by an outsourced service provider.

In every single breach, the **owner of the data** and the **custodian of the data** are not the same party.

You hand over your data to someone else. And they lose it.

---

## “But I’m Not a Big Corporation”

You might think: These are enterprise incidents. What do they have to do with me? I am just an ordinary person storing photos, documents and scanned contracts on my phone. Why would anyone target my data?

It is true that hackers will not specifically hunt for photos on your mobile device. The real issue is not *who is stealing*, but **where your data resides**.

The cloud storage service you use works on the same basic premise as KEPCO’s *Hanjeon ON*. You upload your files to somebody else’s servers. You do not know where those servers are located. You cannot verify how robust their security controls are. You will not automatically be notified if they get hacked.

The only difference is scale. A breach at a major company makes headlines. If your cloud provider is compromised, you may only receive an email — if they choose to notify you at all.

In 2025, the total number of data breaches worldwide rose by 10.83% year-on-year to 41,644. Fewer than a hundred of those made the news.

The rest happen quietly, and fade quietly from memory.

## A Counterintuitive Question: Do Your Files Really Need to Live “in the Cloud”?

We have been marketed cloud storage for more than a decade. Phone makers urge you to “turn on cloud sync and never lose your photos.” Office software promises “cloud collaboration, access anywhere.” Social media apps offer “automatic cloud backups.”

Everything pushes you to upload files online.

But pause and ask yourself: Do most of the files on your phone genuinely need to be stored remotely?

That photo of your ID card you took last week for administrative purposes should have been deleted once the task was done — yet a copy lingers in the cloud. The rental contract you signed works perfectly fine stored locally — but cloud sync made an extra copy automatically. Your payslip you only need to view once is duplicated across three devices.

These files do not belong “in the cloud.” They belong **in your hands**.

## What DoraCloud Sets Out to Achieve

DoraCloud is built around a simple philosophy: **Your files should stay on your own devices.**

It is not a conventional cloud drive. It will not distribute your uploads across data centers around the globe, use AI to scan your file contents for recommendation tuning, or silently back up your photo album in the background.

It is a local-first file management system. Private data and regular files are separated, with an isolated secure zone for sensitive documents. You retain physical oversight of your files: you know where they are stored, who can access them, and whether copies have been made.

This is not an outright rejection of cloud technology. The cloud has clear value for teamwork and collaboration. What DoraCloud pushes back against is the default habit of uploading everything to the cloud indiscriminately, under the false assumption that it is safe.

Files you need to share and collaborate on can go online. Files you want to keep private stay close to you.

Those 1.52 million KEPCO records would not have leaked if they had not been centrally stored on an internet‑reachable server.

That said, that is an enterprise problem, beyond the control of individual users. But for your own files — contracts, identity documents, personal photos, financial records — you can choose a place where they never have to leave your custody.

## Closing Thoughts

The most paradoxical thing about data security is this: Before a breach, nobody thinks their data is valuable. Once the breach hits, people realize their data is no longer theirs.

After 1.52 million records leaked, people started searching forums for their names.

I hope you are not one of them.

More than that, I hope your name was never sitting on that server in the first place.
