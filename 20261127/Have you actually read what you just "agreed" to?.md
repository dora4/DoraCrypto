# Have you actually read what you just "agreed" to?

## — The truth behind the 60th round of regulatory notices

Let’s start with a few figures.

On November 23, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) released its 7th notice of 2026 — the 60th overall batch — naming apps violating user rights. Twenty‑three apps and SDKs were cited, mainly for unauthorized pop‑ups for commercial content with erratic redirects and no viable close option.

Earlier that month, on November 16, the Cybersecurity Administration of MIIT issued a notice flagging 46 apps with prominent fraud risks: these platforms were being exploited by criminals to carry out telecom and online scams.

60 batches.
Do you grasp what this number means? It means MIIT has now issued 60 rounds of public notices targeting apps that infringe on user rights. Every round names anywhere from a dozen to dozens of apps. Each notice states: “Rectification shall be carried out in accordance with relevant regulations. Failure to rectify properly will lead to legal penalties.”

Then the next batch comes out.

## You might think this has nothing to do with you

“I don’t install sketchy apps.”
“I only use apps from big companies.”
“They’re just pop‑ups; I can close them.”

I get that mindset. But here is something you may not know.

Every major app on your phone requested a long list of permissions during installation: access to contacts, photo album, location, camera, microphone, file storage, clipboard — and you tapped “Allow.”

Do you know what it does with your contact list?
You don’t.
Do you know whether it reads your photos to “recognize images” or to “profile your spending habits”?
You don’t.
Do you know if it accesses your clipboard to check for links, or to peek at the bank card number you just copied?
Still no.

The only thing you know for sure: you hit “Allow.” Because without granting permission, you cannot use the app.

---

## So what can we do?

Frankly, in an ecosystem where denial of permission locks you out, ordinary users have almost no bargaining power. You cannot abandon your phone, social media or mobile payments entirely just to protect privacy.

But there is one thing you **can** do: limit the damage if a leak happens.

Imagine the worst‑case scenario: one app on your phone sells your data. What gets sold?
If you saved a screenshot of your bank card password, your password gets sold. If you kept a handwritten photo of your crypto seed phrase, the access key to your crypto wallet gets sold. If you stored the original copy of a contract, your business information gets sold.

But what if those sensitive files on your phone are **encrypted**?

What will the app see? Gibberish. A string of meaningless characters. Without the decryption key, it cannot make any use of them.

That is the point of encryption. It does not stop files from being read outright — on today’s mobile systems, it is nearly impossible to block an app from accessing your files. Encryption stops the data from being **understood**.

You may get hold of my file, but you cannot make sense of it. That is what matters.

## DoraBox: Lock down your sensitive data

DoraBox does one clear thing: **it stores the files you need to protect locally on your phone in encrypted form, and only you hold the key.**

It is not just another file manager. It does not try to be an all‑in‑one tool. It solves one core problem: when your mobile environment cannot be fully trusted (and no mobile environment is perfectly trustworthy), your critical data remains unreadable to outsiders.

How it works:
First, it does not rely on custom‑built encryption algorithms. It uses standard encryption schemes vetted by the global security community. Rolling your own encryption is a major red flag in cybersecurity — home‑grown “locks” almost always contain vulnerabilities.

Second, key management stays local. Your decryption key is never uploaded to any server. A breach of the service provider will not expose your key. The key stays on your device, and only your device.

Third, the interface is minimal. Open the app, select files, encrypt, done. No unnecessary permission requests, no background data uploads, no data collection “to improve user experience.” An encryption tool has no need for your location, contacts or browsing history.

## Who needs DoraBox?

To be honest, not everyone.

If your phone only holds memes and food delivery screenshots, you do not need encrypted storage.

But you may want to consider it if any of the following applies:
You store work files — contracts, quotations, client details. A leak may harm your company, yet you bear the responsibility.
You store financial records — bank card numbers, tax documents, investment screenshots. If leaked, these are not merely “viewed”; they can be abused.
You hold crypto assets — seed phrases, private key screenshots, exchange account details. In 2026, crypto assets are real property vulnerable to theft. Earlier this year in South Korea, cases were reported where asset theft stemmed from leaked photos of seed phrases.
You have private records — things you want no one else to see. Everyone has those. No further explanation needed.

## Back to the original question

Have you actually read what you just “agreed” to?

You clicked accept when the app asked you to approve its privacy policy. You tapped allow when it requested storage access. You swiped past the notice saying “we collect the following information to deliver better services” without reading a word.

It is not your fault. Privacy policies are longer and harder to parse than insurance contracts. And even if you understood every line, you only have two options: agree, or stop using the app.

Still, among all those files you have consented to let apps read, some can be encrypted.

Let those apps read nothing but unintelligible garble.

It is the last bit of control you can hold onto in a world where using an app means surrendering permissions.
