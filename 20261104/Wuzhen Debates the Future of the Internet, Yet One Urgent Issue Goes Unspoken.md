# Wuzhen Debates the Future of the Internet, Yet One Urgent Issue Goes Unspoken

## — On the opening day of the World Internet Conference, let’s talk about those “unprotected” files sitting in your cloud drive

---

Today marks the opening of the 2026 World Internet Conference Wuzhen Summit.

Premier Li Qiang delivered a keynote address, while delegates from across the globe discussed “Digital-Intelligent Empowerment for Innovative Development” — artificial intelligence, data governance, the digital economy, and cybersecurity. Every agenda item is sweeping, tied to national strategies and industry trajectories.

Yet there is one tiny problem unlikely to appear on any panel agenda:
**Is that photo of your ID card stored in your phone’s cloud drive truly safe?**

It sounds too trivial for an international summit. Still, it hits closer to home than any of those macro topics.

---

## Your Cloud Drive May Be the Thing You Understand Least

Let’s run a quick self-check.

Open the cloud drive app you use daily, and ask yourself:

- Whose servers host your files?
- Are your files stored in plaintext or encrypted on those servers?
- If encrypted, who holds the encryption keys?
- Will your files leak if the provider suffers a hack?
- If the provider shuts down unexpectedly, will your files still exist?

Five questions. If you cannot answer a single one — rest assured, 95% of users are in the same boat.

We rely on cloud drives nonstop: automatic photo backups, cross-device document syncing, file sharing with colleagues and clients. Few pause to ask: **What exactly is this “cloud”?**

The simple answer: someone else’s servers.

You hand your files over to a third-party computer and trust it to keep them confidential.

---

## How Fragile That Trust Really Is

Data security is one of the hot topics at today’s summit. Delegates are discussing cross-border data flows, compliance of AI training datasets, and technical frameworks for privacy protection.

These conversations matter because real-world incidents are sounding the alarm.

Just three days ago, on November 1, Binance, the world’s largest cryptocurrency exchange, was hacked in an incident involving assets worth $6.3 billion. Attackers reportedly gained system privileges by compromising the laptop of an operations engineer.

$6.3 billion. This was no small startup, no makeshift operation. It is the world’s leading platform, staffed by top security teams and equipped with premium security infrastructure.

And it was breached anyway.

You might say, “I don’t trade crypto; this has nothing to do with me.”

Here is the connection: **If the world’s largest platform cannot safeguard its data, how secure do you think your free cloud storage really is?**

The only difference: Binance hacks make headline news. You may never even receive a notification if your cloud drive gets compromised.

---

## A Trend You May Have Overlooked

Data security in 2026 bears a new hallmark: **Attackers are no longer only hunting high-value targets — they are mass-scanning ordinary users.**

What does that mean?

In the past, hackers pursued clear targets: large corporations, government bodies, financial institutions. The return on investment was high.

Today, AI has drastically cut attack costs. AI-generated phishing emails can be blasted out to millions of recipients at scale. AI deepfakes can mimic anyone’s voice and video. AI-powered malware automatically hunts for and exploits system vulnerabilities.

The outcome: **Individual users are becoming prime targets for AI-fueled attacks.**
Not necessarily because your data is immensely valuable, but because attacking you costs nearly nothing — and ID numbers, bank details and phone numbers always find buyers on underground markets.

IBM’s annual Cost of a Data Breach Report notes that the global average cost of a breach hit $4.99 million in 2026, with AI-driven attacks growing faster than any other vector.

In this climate, “my cloud drive has a password” is no longer a viable security strategy.

---

## So What Can We Do?

Here is a different framing: **The core issue is not which provider runs more secure servers, but whether your files remain under your control.**

An analogy: If you store gold inside a bank vault, no matter how tight the security or thick the vault door, you still have to trust the bank. If the gold sits inside a safe at your home, and only you hold the key — security does not depend on any third party.

The same logic applies to your files.

The question is not whether your cloud provider does a good job with security. It is this: **Can the provider itself open your files?**

If yes — the provider becomes your biggest vulnerability. Not because it intends to snoop, but because a successful breach hands attackers both your files and the keys to unlock them.

If no — even if the provider’s servers are thoroughly compromised, hackers only obtain meaningless ciphertext. Without the key, encrypted data is just noise.
