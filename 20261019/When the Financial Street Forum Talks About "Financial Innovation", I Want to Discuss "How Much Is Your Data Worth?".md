# When the Financial Street Forum Talks About "Financial Innovation", I Want to Discuss "How Much Is Your Data Worth?"

Today marks the opening of the 2026 Financial Street Forum Annual Conference in Beijing.

More than 400 guests from over 40 countries and regions around the world have gathered for the event, themed *Openness, Cooperation, Inclusiveness — Jointly Building a New Ecosystem for Global Financial Development*. Four heads of financial regulatory authorities are in attendance: Pan Gongsheng, Governor of the People’s Bank of China; Ding Xiangqun, Director of the State Financial Regulatory Administration; Wu Qing, Chairman of the China Securities Regulatory Commission; and Zhu Hexin, Administrator of the State Administration of Foreign Exchange. They will deliver speeches on topics including artificial intelligence and financial innovation, as well as cross-border finance.

On the same day, the World Bank Global AI and Digital Summit kicks off in Seoul. The 33rd APEC Finance Ministers’ Meeting will be held in Hong Kong tomorrow.

These high-level forums address macro-level issues of **data governance** and **digital security**. But as an ordinary individual, I’d like to explore a micro-level question: **How much are the files you store in your cloud drive actually worth? Who bears responsibility for their safety?**

## An Account You May Never Have Calculated

Let’s start with a thought experiment.

Suppose all the data on your phone — photos, chat records, documents, memos — is packaged into a "personal data profile". What value would you assign to it?

IBM’s 2026 Cost of a Data Breach Report offers a reference figure: the average global cost of data breaches rose by 12% year-on-year, hitting an all-time high. This cost covers detection, response, notification, legal fees and business losses. However, this calculation is from a corporate perspective.

What about from an individual’s standpoint? There is the time and effort spent reissuing identity documents after ID photos leak; financial losses from credit card fraud following exposure of banking information; potential insurance discrimination if your medical records are compromised. None of these costs are covered by cloud storage providers.

In fact, most cloud drive user agreements clearly state: **"The platform shall not be liable for data leaks caused by force majeure or third-party acts."**

You pay for storage space, but security guarantees are never included in that price.

## Where Your Files Reside, There Lies Your Security

Most people understand "cloud storage" as an invisible place to keep files.

But the "cloud" is not something floating in the sky. It is physical hardware: a server inside a company’s data center. Once you upload files to the cloud, physical control over them passes to that company.

The company has employees, commercial interests and legal obligations. Your files can be exposed if hackers breach its defenses, insiders misuse access, or legal authorities demand data handover.

This is no hypothetical scenario.

At the end of September, five major commercial banks in South Korea suffered synchronized AI-powered hacking attacks. Around 25,000 customers of Shinhan Bank had their names, phone numbers, annual incomes and loan limits stolen. Twenty-five hours passed from the start of the attack until the bank detected it.

On October 16, German travel platform Skyticket reported a cyberattack, in which credit card information of 6,140 users was leaked. The compromised data came from old payment logs dating back to 2017–2018. Left undeleted during data migration, the records sat quietly on servers for nearly a decade before being stolen.

**Data you store on the cloud does not become safer over time. On the contrary, it turns into a forgotten ticking time bomb.**

## The DoraCloud Approach: Encryption Stays in Your Hands

DoraCloud aims to answer a fundamental question: **What if you cannot fully trust third parties to hold your stored data?**

Its solution can be summed up in three words: **Tiered Management.**

**First, client-side encryption.** Files are encrypted on your local device before upload. Encryption keys remain solely in your possession. DoraCloud’s servers only receive ciphertext. Even if the server is breached, attackers can only obtain encrypted data that cannot be decrypted. This differs fundamentally from mainstream cloud drives, where files are encrypted by the platform after upload.

**Second, partitioned management.** DoraCloud features two built-in spaces: a General Zone and a Privacy Zone. Work documents and family photos go into the General Zone for easy daily synchronization and sharing. Scanned identity documents, bank passwords, contracts and medical reports are stored in the Privacy Zone, protected by independent encryption and separate access controls. Much like having ordinary drawers and a safe at home, not everything should be kept in the same place.

**Third, decentralized storage architecture.** Files are split into fragments, encrypted, and dispersed across multiple nodes. No single node can reconstruct the complete file. This eliminates the centralized risk of "one key opening all locks".

## Three Things You Can Do Starting Today

Regardless of which cloud storage tool you use, take these three steps right away:

**1. Tier your files.** Open your current cloud drive or phone storage and categorize your files into three groups: publicly shareable content (landscape photos, public documents), moderately private content (personal notes, work materials), and highly sensitive content (IDs, passwords, financial records). Never mix the third category with the second.

**2. Check your cloud drive settings.** See whether automatic backup is enabled by default. You may be syncing contacts, call logs and even app data to the cloud without realizing it. Turn off synchronization options you do not need.

**3. Keep local copies of important files.** The cloud should never be your sole storage copy. Maintain at least one local backup on hardware under your physical control.

## While Forums Discuss Big Issues, You Can Take Small Action

The "financial data security" and "cross-border data flows" debated at the Financial Street Forum may sound distant to ordinary people. Down to the individual level, however, they boil down to one core question: **Who should control data?**

For nations, the answer is data sovereignty. For enterprises, it is data compliance. For individuals, the answer is simple: **Your files should be encrypted by you, safeguarded by you, and shared only at your discretion.**
