# 68,000 South Korean Bank Customers Taught Me This: These Files Should Never Be on Your Phone

## — An AI-Driven Hacking Storm and a Digital Box Full of Secrets

Ms. Kim, a long-time customer of Shinhan Bank in Seoul, received a text message on the afternoon of October 1. It said her name, phone number and annual income were no longer held securely by the bank.

“What do you mean they no longer have my information?” she told Yonhap News Agency. “The place where I’ve kept my savings my whole life can’t even protect my name?”

Ms. Kim was one of the 68,000 account holders affected by the hacking storm hitting South Korea’s financial sector. From late September to early October, seven major South Korean financial institutions — Shinhan Bank, KB Kookmin Bank, Hana Bank, Woori Bank, NH Nonghyup Bank, BNK Busan Bank and Jeil Savings Bank — were compromised almost simultaneously. Attackers leveraged AI-powered automated tools to scan for vulnerabilities, test credentials and expand their attack surface. The whole operation unfolded so rapidly that the banks’ security teams could barely keep up.

Shinhan Bank saw data leakage for 25,000 customers. Credit card records of 119 people were exposed at Kookmin Bank. Jeil Savings Bank had the names, birth dates and phone numbers of 40,000 users stolen.

South Korean President Lee Jae-myung noted during a cabinet meeting on October 6: “There are signs of AI being used in some hacking incidents.”

What he did not say out loud was this: **While attackers are armed with AI, defenders are still relying on last-century tools.**

## What’s Stored on Your Phone?

I won’t dive deep into an analysis of the banks’ security flaws. Instead, let’s talk about something more personal.

Right now, open your phone’s file manager and look through what you keep there:
Photos of the front and back of your ID card? Yes.
Scanned copy of your driver’s license? Yes.
Screenshots of your bank card numbers? Yes.
PDFs of medical checkup reports? Yes.
That memo with all your account usernames and passwords? Yes.

These files sit quietly in your phone storage, mixed with food delivery cache, short video temporary files and WeChat chat images. Your phone holds a vault containing your most private information — yet the vault door is only guarded by a six-digit lock screen passcode.

A friend I met last year had his phone stolen on the subway. He remotely locked it within ten minutes and changed every password he could in twenty minutes. But before the lock kicked in, the thief had already exported all sensitive files from his photo album and file manager.

Later, he discovered someone had applied for a small loan on an online platform using photos of his ID card.

These files deserve far safer storage. But where can you keep them securely?

## What Can a Box Do?

The DoraBox is built to answer exactly this question.

It is not an all-in-one security suite, nor a fancy cloud sync tool. It does one core thing: **place your phone’s most sensitive files inside a genuine digital safe.**

Here is how it works:
**AES-256 Encryption.** This is the encryption standard widely adopted by financial institutions and militaries worldwide. Once your ID photos, bank credentials and medical reports are placed inside DoraBox, they are turned into ciphertext that can only be decoded with the correct key. Even if someone gets hold of your phone and bypasses the lock screen, all they will see is meaningless garbled data.

**Zero-Knowledge Architecture.** DoraBox servers never store your encryption keys. That means even DoraBox engineers cannot open your box. This is not just a promise — it is baked into the technical design. Keys are generated and stored locally on your device and are never uploaded.

**Biometric Unlock.** Fingerprint or facial recognition serves as a quick unlock method. Your biometric data also stays only on your local device.

**Decoy Vault.** This feature is invaluable in extreme scenarios. You can set a decoy password; entering it opens a fake vault filled with trivial files. If you are coerced into unlocking your phone, you can reveal this fake vault instead.

## What Should Go Into the Box?

Not every file needs encrypted storage. But these categories are strongly recommended:
**Identity Documents.** Scans of ID cards, passports, driver’s licenses and household registration booklets. Replacing these documents after loss is cumbersome, and data exposure carries far greater risks — ID photos can be used for real-name verification across many platforms.

**Financial Records.** Bank account numbers, insurance policies, tax documents and payslips. The South Korean bank breach shows that even financial institutions struggle to protect such data; screenshots saved on your phone are even more vulnerable.

**Medical Records.** Health check reports, medical histories and prescription records. This information is far more sensitive than most people realize. If insurers, employers or even potential dates gain access, the consequences can be uncomfortable.

**Passwords & Keys.** Account passwords, crypto wallet seed phrases, SSH keys and API keys. These are the keys to your digital life and should never be stored in plain text inside photo albums or mobile memos.

**Personal Files.** Journals, letters and private records you want to keep away from everyone else. You have the right to own things that belong only to you.

## Back to Those 68,000 Korean Bank Customers

South Korea’s Financial Supervisory Service found during its investigation that attackers targeted the banks’ peripheral systems: staff remote office platforms and business support systems, whose security protections are far weaker than core transaction systems.

This mirrors the risk on your own phone: your high-value sensitive data (bank passwords, ID photos) lives alongside peripheral storage spaces (albums, memos, file managers) under the same level of protection. You may think your lock screen is a solid defense, but it only stops amateur thieves.

DoraBox creates an independent security barrier inside your phone. Even if your phone itself is compromised, the box remains secure.
