# The State Is Setting Rules for Your Data. But There’s One Thing It Cannot Regulate.

——After the Regulation on Government Data Sharing Takes Effect: Who Will Safeguard Your Personal Files?

On November 3, the State Council issued an order.
The *Regulation on Government Data Sharing* was officially released. While the title sounds bureaucratic, it addresses an issue that affects everyone:
**How government departments share your data among themselves.**

Your household registration records, social security files, real estate registrations, tax ratings, administrative penalties — these records are scattered across dozens of agencies. Previously, cross-department data exchange relied on negotiation and coordination; requests often stalled when parties could not reach an agreement. Now a legal framework is in place: who may access data, how it may be used, how it should be stored afterward, and who bears liability for breaches. Every detail is written into black-and-white rules.

Three core principles run through the regulation: **security, orderliness, and efficiency.**
Security comes first.

That makes sense. Government data touches on national security and personal privacy, so strict controls are mandatory. Yet after reading the full text, one question arises:
**Rules now govern government data. What about your own data?**

## Your Personal Data Is Far Less Regulated

Think about where your daily data ends up:
WeChat chat logs — on Tencent’s servers. Alipay transaction records — on Ant Group’s servers. Cloud files — on Baidu Netdisk, Alibaba Cloud Drive, iCloud. Phone photos — local storage plus iCloud. Work emails — on corporate mail servers. Food delivery orders — on Meituan and Ele.me servers. Ride-hailing history — on Didi’s servers.

Your entire digital life is chopped into dozens of fragments, hosted across disparate systems run by many companies.
**No single regulation dictates how these firms share your data with one another.**

More importantly, there is no unified way for you to manage all of this data yourself.
At minimum, government data is bounded by this sharing regulation. Your personal data? Often you cannot even view a complete copy of it.

Have you ever tried exporting, compiling, and centrally managing all your data across platforms?
Most people have not. Those who try quickly hit roadblocks: some platforms offer no export function; some export proprietary, unopenable formats; some only return records from the past 30 days; many never tell you exactly how much of your information they hold.

**Government data is moving toward orderly sharing. Personal data remains in a state of unregulated wild growth.**

## A Change You May Have Overlooked

November 4, the day after the government data sharing regulation was published, the Beijing Communications Administration released its 11th circular.
Eight apps were publicly cited for “collecting personal information in violation of the necessity principle” and “failing to clearly state the purposes, methods and scope of data collection and usage”, and ordered to rectify within a deadline.

On the same day, the Supreme People’s Procuratorate released a batch of guiding cases on protecting citizens’ personal information. One case involved doxxing and cyber harassment: attackers illegally obtained personal information to identify victims and launch targeted online abuse.

The regulation constrains government bodies. The circular disciplines tech firms. The cases deter criminal actors.
**Yet all these measures are external forms of oversight. They govern how others handle your data.**

One area remains outside regulatory reach: **how you manage your own data.**
It is your call whether to store ID scans alongside selfies in the same folder. Whether to let your cloud drive’s AI assistant “learn” your contracts to refine recommendations. Whether to save medical reports and travel guides under one cloud account.

No regulation makes these choices for you. The decisions are yours alone.
And most people choose inaction: dumping everything into one place and pretending the risks do not exist.

## Why Segmentation Matters

The *Regulation on Government Data Sharing* rests on a core design principle worth adopting for personal use: **classification and grading.**

Government data is sorted into tiers: freely shareable, conditionally shareable, and non-shareable. Each tier comes with distinct access permissions, usage rules and security requirements.

This logic applies equally to personal data management.
Your files naturally fall into two categories:

**General files.** Travel photos, music libraries, video collections, work slides, study notes, e-books. If lost, they are inconvenient; if exposed, they may be embarrassing, but they pose no critical risk. Their priority is **convenience**: fast syncing, cross-device access, high-capacity storage, AI-powered sorting.

**Sensitive private files.** ID scans, bank statements, original contracts, medical reports, tax records, password notes. If lost or leaked, they can trigger direct financial harm or safety risks. Their priority is **security**: end-to-end encryption, user-held keys, zero-knowledge storage.

These two sets of files have conflicting requirements. Mixing them is like keeping cash and snacks in the same drawer: anyone grabbing snacks can easily spot the cash, and casual browsing exposes sensitive material.
**Segmentation is the starting point of sensible data management.**

## Lessons From the CIIE

Right now in Shanghai, the 9th China International Import Expo is underway.
Since its opening on November 7, cross-border data flows have been a central topic for global political and business leaders. The second meeting of the China-EU Trade and Investment Consultation Mechanism was held in Beijing, and its joint statement referenced security controls for outbound data. The Cyberspace Administration of China released Q&A documents on outbound data security rules in September, clarifying compliance pathways for enterprises.

Data flows. Data is shared. Data is analyzed, trained on and repurposed.
This is inevitable in the digital economy. Stagnant data means stagnant growth. The government data sharing regulation unlocks government data flows. The CIIE debates international data flows. Large AI models demand training data flows.

**Flow is beneficial, yet some data should never flow freely.**
