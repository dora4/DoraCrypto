## Three Real-World Cases and the Files on Your Phone

### Case 1: Over 10 Million Student Records Trafficked for 0.5 Yuan Apiece

Since April 2026, more than 10 million personal records of primary and secondary school students across Jiangsu, Guangdong, Zhejiang, Anhui, Shandong, Liaoning and other regions have been illegally obtained and resold. The data includes student names, grades, classes, parents’ phone numbers, ID numbers and home addresses. Individual records sell for between 0.5 yuan and more than ten yuan.

The source? Official education information platforms and related systems.

Parents keep receiving targeted cold calls from tutoring agencies, who can accurately state their child’s school, class and even home address.

### Case 2: Google Exposed Digital Sex Crime Victims’ Data in South Korea

In September 2026, Google shared deletion requests submitted by victims of digital sex crimes in South Korea directly with an overseas research project website. This exposed victims’ names, ages, workplaces, schools, mobile numbers and other personal information online. The victims’ desperate pleas such as “I am in extreme pain” and “please save me” were published without anonymization.

South Korea’s Ministry of Gender Equality and Family said it was considering pressing criminal charges against Google, which issued a public apology.

### Case 3: Times Car Japan Leaked Data of 6.6 Million Users

On September 29, 2026, Times Car, Japan’s largest car-sharing provider, disclosed a breach affecting roughly 6.6 million users, including scanned copies of around 1.6 million driver’s licenses. The secondary risk of identity theft raised serious concerns.

These three incidents share one core trait:
**What was leaked was no top-secret material, but ordinary data generated in everyday life.**
Student information, ID documents, driver licenses — you may have all of these stored on your phone.

## 5-Minute Self-Audit Checklist

Grab your phone and start right now:

### Step 1: Gallery Scan (2 minutes)

Open your photo album and search for these keywords:

- “ID card”
- “Bank card”
- “Contract”
- “Password”
- “Certificate” (birth certificate, residence certificate, etc.)

Count how many results come up. Where are these photos stored? Have they been synced to the cloud? Were they sent via WeChat (recipients may retain copies even after you delete yours)?

### Step 2: App Permission Review (1 minute)

Go to System Settings → App Permissions and check:

- Which apps have access to read your photo gallery?
- Which apps can read local files?
- Why does a flashlight app need gallery access? Why does a food delivery app request your contacts?

One key focus of the 2026 special campaign for personal information protection is cracking down on excessive data collection by apps. But regulation takes time — your data will not wait.

### Step 3: Cloud Storage Inventory (2 minutes)

Review your cloud drives and cloud backup services:

- Are there backups of sensitive files such as ID cards or contracts?
- Are these files encrypted?
- Do you know the cloud provider’s encryption policy, and who holds the encryption keys?

### Scenario 1: Hotel Wi-Fi

You connect to public hotel Wi-Fi and work on files you think are “not important”. Public Wi-Fi is a breeding ground for man-in-the-middle attacks. Unencrypted data you transmit can be intercepted by others on the same network.

DoraBox’s protection: Even on insecure networks, your sensitive files remain inside the encrypted vault; only ciphertext is transmitted.

DoraBox’s protection: Data inside the encrypted container uses AES-256 encryption. Even if attackers retrieve raw chip data, they cannot decrypt it without your key.
