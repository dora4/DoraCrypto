# What’s the first thing that crosses your mind when your phone is lost?

## —It’s not the device itself, but the irreplaceable data stored inside it

### Minute 0

The subway pulls into the station. You stand up, reach into your pocket, and find nothing.

Your heart skips a beat. You rummage through your backpack, check every coat pocket three times, even squat down to look under the seat.

Your phone is gone.

### Minute 5

Standing in the subway station, you start running through a mental checklist:
Banking apps — protected by fingerprint login, but you ticked “remember password” last time.
WeChat — still logged in, with full chat history saved locally.
Photo album — photos from your father’s hospital stay last year, your child’s vaccination record, front and back scans of your ID card.
Notes app — all your passwords, security questions, bank account numbers, saved here so you wouldn’t forget them.
File manager — scanned contract from a client last month, not yet backed up to your computer.

It suddenly dawns on you: what you lost is not merely a phone. It is a **compact database**, holding nearly everything important in your life.

### Minute 30

You borrow a stranger’s phone to make emergency calls.

Mobile carrier support: Your SIM card has been suspended. To get a replacement, you need to visit an outlet with your ID.

Bank support: Your bank card is temporarily frozen. But any transactions authorized via quick payment before freezing will need to be verified one by one.

WeChat support: To freeze your account, you need your registered mobile number, ID card, and friend verification… but your mobile number has already been suspended.

You jump between different customer service lines. Each requires identity verification — yet the very materials you need for verification are trapped inside that lost phone.

### Hour 2

You sit at the police station giving a statement.

The officer asks whether your phone contained sensitive data. You say yes. He asks if the data was encrypted. You pause… The photo album is unencrypted, the notes app is unencrypted, and the file manager is unencrypted.

The officer’s expression says it all.

## How much of your data sits “unprotected” on your phone

Let’s run a self-check. Open your phone right now and see where you store these items:

**Photos of identity documents** — most likely mixed in your regular photo gallery. Anyone or any app with access to your gallery can view them.
**Photos or screenshots of bank cards** — also possibly stored in your album. Some people even write passwords in photo captions.
**Various passwords and security answers** — saved in notes, sticky notes, WeChat File Transfer Assistant, or even emails sent to yourself.
**Contracts and financial documents** — buried in the “downloads” folder of your file manager alongside random downloaded files.
**Private keys and seed phrases** — if you work with crypto assets, these may exist as screenshots or text files tucked away in an obscure folder.

All of this data shares one critical trait: **if your phone falls into the wrong hands, all of it becomes exposed.**
This is not necessarily due to a hacker attack. It happens because the data was never encrypted in the first place.

## Most people do not refuse protection; they just do not know how to implement it

For a long time, encryption has been framed as something only tech enthusiasts can handle.

You need to understand AES-256. You need to know how to generate strong encryption keys. You need to follow key management best practices. You need to tell which apps claim encryption but do not truly deliver it.

These barriers are too high for ordinary users. So most people simply skip encryption. They dump all their sensitive information into an unprotected folder and hope their phone never gets lost.

## A counterintuitive truth

Many people assume: “My phone has a lock screen password, so it is secure.”

But a lock screen password only guards **entry to the whole phone**, not individual areas within it. Once the lock screen is bypassed — via security vulnerabilities, flashing firmware, or SIM card attacks — every piece of data on the phone becomes exposed.

DoraBox provides **a second lock**. Even if the first lock is breached, the second one operates independently. Its encryption keys are not stored within the phone’s lock screen system, but in its own dedicated security module.

It is like having two layers of security at home: your front door and a safe. A burglar may pry open the front door, but the safe remains sealed.
