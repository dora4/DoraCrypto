# Anhui Released a List, Exposing a Question You May Never Have Considered

— There is a “negative list” for cross-border data transfers. Do you have a “zoning list” for your files?

---

Yesterday, November 11, the Cyberspace Administration of Anhui, together with three other authorities, officially issued a document.

Its full name is quite lengthy: *Measures for the Administration of the Negative List for Cross-Border Data Transfers in the China (Anhui) Pilot Free Trade Zone (Trial)* and the supporting *Negative List (2026 Version)*.

The core idea is straightforward:
**A list specifies which data may not be transferred out of the mainland; items not on the list may be exported after compliance procedures.**

This is not an Anhui-only initiative. From the Cyberspace Administration of China to various free trade zones, “negative lists for cross-border data transfers” are becoming a standard regulatory instrument in 2026. On September 11, the CAC released the latest *Q&A on Policies and Regulations for the Security Administration of Cross-Border Data Transfers*. The message is clear:
**Data flow must have boundaries.**

Governments manage outbound data with lists. Enterprises classify internal data through tiered systems.

But turn to yourself as an individual —
**Do you have a list for your files?**

---

## An Uncomfortable Truth

Open your go-to cloud drive or file manager.

Look at its structure.

It most likely looks like this: one folder for work, one for life, one for photos. With luck, you may also have an “important” folder.

Now browse your “life” folder: travel itineraries, ID scans, recipes, bank statements, children’s medical reports, rental contracts, last year’s tax records — all mixed together.

**Do you have any awareness of zoning?**
Probably yes, because you sorted them into folders.

**But is this zoning effective?**
No. You grouped files by *topic*, not by *security level*.

A travel guide and an ID scan sit under the same “life” folder. They may both relate to life, yet their security requirements are worlds apart. If you lose a travel guide, you can simply search for another. If your ID scan leaks, you could spend the next year fighting identity theft.

The state uses negative lists to tier data: what can move freely and what must stay within the border.

Your file management still relies on crude topic-based folders.

---

## Why Grouping by Topic Is Not Security Tiering

The logic is easy to grasp.

Think of your refrigerator: raw meat and cooked food are stored separately. You do not pile them together just because both are food. Their preservation needs and cross-contamination risks differ.

The same applies to your files.

**Ordinary files** — photos, videos, work slides, travel plans, recipes. If exposed, they rarely cause material harm. You access them often, need fast syncing across devices, and share them easily.

**Sensitive private data** — ID photos, bank card screenshots, contract scans, medical records, tax documents, password notes. A leak carries severe consequences. You access them infrequently, yet they are highly critical. They must never be viewed by unauthorized parties.

These two categories have entirely different security requirements. Storing them in the same place under identical protection leaves you with only bad tradeoffs: encrypt everything (cumbersome, slow sync, you will abandon it quickly) or encrypt nothing (convenient, but completely exposed).

This is the dilemma most people face:
**Either overprotected to the point of unusability, or underprotected and effectively unguarded.**

## Inspiration from a Single List

At its core, Anhui’s cross-border negative list solves exactly this problem:
**Not all data carries equal risk, so one set of rules cannot govern everything.**

Data eligible for free movement can flow freely.
Data that requires containment is tightly controlled.
Data falling in the middle can flow under defined conditions.

Applied to personal file management, this is the design philosophy behind DoraCloud:
**Not every file needs encryption. Nor can every file be left unprotected. The key is proper zoning.**

## Why This Has Become Urgent in 2026

Three trends are turning file zoning from “nice to have” into “must-have”:

**First: AI agents need access to your files.**
2026 marks the breakout year for AI agents. Your digital assistant sorts emails, archives contracts, and searches old records — and it needs permission to read your files to work. But do you really want it to stumble across your bank statements while organizing vacation photos?

DoraCloud’s zoning lets AI assistants operate freely within the regular zone. Without the key, nothing in the private zone can be opened. Your AI gets work done without seeing what it should not.

**Second: Data breach costs hit an all-time high.**
IBM’s 2026 Cost of a Data Breach Report puts the global average breach cost at $4.99 million, up 12% year over year. A single stolen personal record fetches an average of $188 on underground markets.

While corporations and governments suffer massive losses from breaches, most individual users still treat a single account password as sufficient protection.

**Third: Cross-border data regulation is tightening.**
From CAC’s cross-border data security assessments, to Anhui’s newly released negative list, to the September Q&A document on cross-border data rules — the signal is consistent: data flows need rules, and sensitive data demands stronger safeguards.

The state tiers data through regulation. You should tier your own files too.
