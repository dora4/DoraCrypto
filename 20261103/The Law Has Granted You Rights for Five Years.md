# The Law Has Granted You Rights for Five Years — But Your Files Haven’t Gotten the Memo

## — Five Years of the Personal Information Protection Law: The Gap Between Knowing and Doing

---

Yesterday marked a moment worth quiet reflection.

November 1 was the fifth anniversary of the official implementation of the **Personal Information Protection Law of the People’s Republic of China**.

On that day five years ago, this statute put it in black and white for every Chinese citizen: **you are in control of your personal information**. Collection requires notification, usage requires consent, storage must be limited, and breaches carry liability for compensation.

On that same day this year, the Supreme People’s Procuratorate released a set of guiding cases, a progress report for these five years: eight criminal cases involving infringement of citizens’ personal information, covering courier waybills, hospital medical records, delivery addresses, facial data and more.

If you put these two facts side by side, an awkward truth emerges:
**The law has been in force for five years, yet most people’s data protection awareness is still stuck at the starting line.**

---

## How Much Has the Landscape Shifted in Eight Years

Let’s rewind further.

On 25 May 2018, the EU GDPR took effect — the world’s first truly comprehensive general data protection regulation. On launch day, Meta (Facebook) and Google each received multi-billion-euro complaints.

Eight years on, more than 140 countries and regions worldwide have enacted personal information protection laws. From Europe to South America, Southeast Asia to Africa, the principle that **your data belongs to you** has evolved from an ideal held by a few into legislation across most nations.

China has moved at a brisk pace. Its 2021 Personal Information Protection Law is stricter than the GDPR in many respects: restrictions on automated decision-making, dedicated safeguards for sensitive personal information, and review requirements for cross-border data transfers.

Still, law is law.
Reality is reality.

## What Those Eight Cases Tell Us

I won’t unpack every Supreme Procuratorate case in full, but here are a few patterns to illustrate the risk:
Insiders at courier firms bulk-export users’ addresses and phone numbers for sale. The waybill on every parcel you receive may become merchandise on the dark web.
Backdoors planted in hospital systems steal entire databases of patient medical records. Your most private health history may be priced and traded line by line on some remote server.
Facial imagery is captured without authorisation and fed into commercial systems. Your face, a side profile captured while you walk down the street or enter your residential compound, may already be tagged, packaged and resold.

These are not movie plots. They are active prosecutorial cases from 2026.

Behind each case sit hundreds — sometimes millions — of ordinary people whose information is collected, used and traded without their knowledge. Most never even realise their rights have been violated.

**The law protects your rights. It cannot store and guard your files for you.**

## One Dividing Line, Two Zones

DoraCloud’s core design is simple:
**It draws a line across your storage space and splits your files into two zones.**

**General File Zone:** Photos, videos, slides, travel notes, work drafts. This zone matches mainstream cloud drive convenience — fast sync, instant multi-device access, simple sharing. Usability remains fully intact.

**Private Data Zone:** ID documents, contracts, financial records, medical files, password notes. Files here travel over an end-to-end encrypted channel: encrypted locally on your device before upload. Decryption keys stay on your devices only. DoraCloud servers hold nothing but ciphertext; no one, including DoraCloud engineers, can read the content.

A clear boundary separates the two zones: the general zone prioritises efficiency, the private zone prioritises security. You do not need to sort manually — DoraCloud identifies file types and prompts you to move sensitive items into private storage. You do not need to memorise extra credentials: one master password suffices, and cryptographically, it belongs only to you.

The design rests on a well-documented behavioural rule: **users will bypass security if it adds constant friction to daily work.**

Full-disk encryption is slow; no encryption at all is reckless. DoraCloud strikes the middle path: keep 80% of everyday files fast and fluid, while locking the 20% high-sensitivity files behind the strongest protection available.

Think of your home: snacks sit on the coffee table, property deeds go in the safe. You do not lock every bag of crisps away, and you do not leave your title deeds scattered on the sofa.

## Who Holds the Keys? A Five-Year-Old Question, Answered Today

When the Personal Information Protection Law was passed five years ago, one debate dominated legal and tech circles: **Has the “consent” model broken?**

Recall that checkbox every app makes you tick: “I have read and agree to the privacy policy.” Did you read it? Almost certainly not. Did you tick it anyway? Yes. That is not meaningful consent. It is coerced acceptance.

Five years later, it is clear consent is hollow in many scenarios. But a deeper principle matters more:
**Control.**

Even where you consent to a platform collecting your data, you retain the legal right to control how that data is used, stored and deleted. That right is written into the Personal Information Protection Law.

Technically, control boils down to one decisive question: **Who holds the keys?**

If your files are encrypted but the service provider keeps the keys, your “control” is nominal. The provider can decrypt them; staff can decrypt them; hackers who steal provider systems can decrypt them. You are told your data is safe — but you do not truly hold the security.

DoraCloud’s Private Data Zone uses a client-side encryption architecture: keys are generated locally on your device and never leave it. Servers store only ciphertext. This is not a promise that “DoraCloud will not look at your files.” It is a technical impossibility for DoraCloud to read them.

Cryptography does not rely on promises. It relies on mathematics.

## The Law Has Pulled Ahead — Are You Keeping Up?

Five years ago, the law drew a line for you: your personal information is yours to control.

This year, the Supreme Procuratorate’s eight cases show the penalties for crossing that line are real, severe and ongoing.

But the law has limits. It can punish wrongdoers, yet it cannot encrypt files for you. It can set rules for data collection, yet it cannot manage your keys. It can oblige companies to run secure systems, yet it cannot choose which tools you use to protect yourself.
