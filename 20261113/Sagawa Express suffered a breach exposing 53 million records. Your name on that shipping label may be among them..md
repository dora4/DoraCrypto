# Sagawa Express suffered a breach exposing 53 million records. Your name on that shipping label may be among them.

**— When “someone else’s problem” becomes everyone’s problem, are your files still safe?**

---

Last month, an incident broke out in Japan.
Sagawa Express, one of Japan’s three major courier companies — comparable to SF Express in China — issued an announcement: its website parcel tracking service was accessed illegally by a third party, and **up to 53 million personal records may have been leaked**.

Fifty-three million.

This is no distant statistic. Sagawa has countless Chinese residents in Japan, cross-border e-commerce sellers, and Japan-China traders among its customers. Every parcel you have shipped carries your name, phone number and address. The electronic pad you sign on for deliveries connects directly to this very system.

Around the same time, Japanese restaurant chains and IT service providers reported successive data breaches. October 2026 practically became Japan’s “month of data leaks.”

You might think: This happened in Japan, so what does it have to do with me?

Here is the connection:
**If a large enterprise serving tens of millions of users with a mature IT infrastructure can lose 53 million records in one breach, what makes you think the place where you store your files is any safer?**

---

## A routine action you take every day, blind to its risks

Open the Files or cloud storage app on your phone.
Chances are it contains these: photos of your ID card front and back, screenshots of bank cards, scanned rental contracts, payslips, last year’s medical checkup reports, your child’s birth certificate.

Now ask yourself:
**Are these kept in the same space as your travel photos, funny memes and food delivery screenshots?**

Most likely, yes.

That is the core problem.

What did Sagawa Express lose? Names, phone numbers and addresses from shipping labels — logistics data. Such data should be isolated in a dedicated system, separated from entertainment and marketing data. When everything is mixed together, one intrusion means everything gets exposed.

The same logic applies to your files. Storing your ID photo alongside travel itineraries means they receive identical protection. And that security level was designed for travel notes, not identity documents.

**You are not lacking security awareness. You just haven’t put that awareness into data segregation.**

---

## Why most people fail to segregate their sensitive data

It is not laziness. Mainstream tools do not give you a proper way to separate risk tiers.

Popular cloud drives follow one model: **one account, one storage pool, all files together**. You can create folders, but every folder shares the same security profile. It is like your home having only one front-door lock — your bedroom and safe both use the same key.

Your only traditional choices are:

- Encrypt everything: slow, cumbersome, abandoned within days
- Encrypt nothing: convenient, but completely exposed

It is a binary choice with no middle ground.

**Segregation is not about making data harder to steal. It makes stolen data useless.**

## Who holds the key — the one thing you need to understand

If you remember only one line from this:
**For encryption, the critical question is not whether something is encrypted. It is who holds the decryption key.**

Major cloud services do encrypt data at rest, but the provider retains the keys. In theory, their engineers can view your files, their AI systems may analyze your data, and keys could be compromised if their systems get hacked.

For DoraCloud’s Private Data Zone, the keys belong solely to you. Not mostly you, not by default — **only you**.

This is not “trusting DoraCloud not to peek.” DoraCloud is technically unable to see your plaintext. Cryptography relies not on trust, but on mathematics.
