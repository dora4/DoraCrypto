# Humanoid Robots: Market to Grow 20x in Five Years — Will Your Phone Still Know You Then?

I came across a news headline today: sober in wording yet mind-bending in content.

*“Research predicts humanoid robots will become major buyers of memory chips, with their market size surging roughly 20 times within five years.”*

Humanoid robots. No longer just a concept from sci-fi movies, but a solid industry forecast. Lisa Su recently proclaimed 2026 the “Year of Inference,” and now we’re told: in five years, robots built in our likeness will tighten bolts on factory floors, hand out medicine in hospitals, and pour water for you at home.

I don’t fear robots themselves. But one thing troubles me — **as more smart devices weave into your life, where will your data flow?**

## Your Phone Already Knows More About You Than Most People

Count the smart devices around you: smartphone, tablet, smartwatch, smart speaker, robot vacuum, smart lock, dashcam, smart TV.

Every single one collects data. Your phone tracks your location history. Your smartwatch logs your heart rate and sleep quality. Your robot vacuum maps the layout of your home. Your smart lock records when you enter and leave.

This data is scattered across different devices, apps and cloud platforms. You have no unified way to manage it, and may not even be able to list exactly where all your data resides.

Now imagine: five years from now, humanoid robots step into your home. To serve you “intelligently,” they need to know your house layout, daily routines, where you keep your belongings, and even the conversations you hold.

Are you comfortable handing over all that information? More precisely — are you willing to give it to the company behind the robot?

## A Real-Life Little Scenario

I moved house last month. While sorting belongings, I found an old USB flash drive. When I opened it, I saw files from five years ago: my graduation thesis, job applications, and photos I’d rather not revisit.

There was nothing special about the drive itself, but its contents were deeply personal. My first reaction was not “Wow, memories!” but: **it would be really awkward if other people saw these.**

Then I checked my phone. It holds far more content than that old USB drive: ID photos, banking screenshots, PDF work contracts, private notes. Stored across photo albums, file explorers and memo apps, most are saved as plaintext.

Your phone knows you — it has your fingerprint, face, password and daily movement patterns. Yet its protection only keeps strangers from unlocking the screen. Once someone bypasses the lock screen (technically not difficult), everything inside is exposed.

## What DoraBox Does

DoraBox solves a straightforward problem: **store your most critical data inside a genuinely secure vault.**

It is not cloud storage, not cloud sync, nor a simple “encrypted folder.” It is a privacy-focused encrypted storage product built from the ground up on zero-trust principles.

Specifically:
**True encryption.** It adopts the AES-256 encryption standard. Encryption keys are generated and stored locally on your device and never leave it. DoraBox’s servers never know your keys. This is not just a promise; it is baked into the architecture. Even DoraBox’s own team cannot access your vault.

**Tiered unlocking.** Biometric authentication (fingerprint / facial recognition) enables quick daily access, while core operations require a password. Biometric data stays local and is never uploaded to any server.

**Decoy vault.** This is a carefully designed feature: you can set a decoy password. Entering it opens a fake vault holding trivial files. If you are forced to unlock your device (e.g. your phone gets stolen), you can hand over the decoy vault instead.

**Local-first design.** Your data resides on your device by default. If synchronization is needed, data is transmitted in encrypted form and decrypted only on the target device. There is no plaintext exposure at any point in the entire pipeline.

## A Sense of Security in the Intelligent Age

Back to humanoid robots.

Five years down the line, when robots enter households, the boundaries of data will blur further. Your robot will know everything about you: sleep cycles, consumption habits, floor plans of your home, every conversation you have with it. If such data is spread across various clouds and platforms, your security defenses will be little more than a paper shield.

DoraBox’s philosophy is simple: **no matter how many smart devices out there collect your data, at least your most sensitive information — IDs, passwords, financial records and private documents — should remain under your control.**

Not stored on some company’s server, not left to hope that “they will protect me.” Instead, technically and tangibly, only you can unlock it.

## What Happened to That USB Drive

I copied the important files from the USB drive into DoraBox and then formatted the drive.

Not for nostalgia, but for practicality. Those files carry sentimental value for me, yet to others they might merely be gossip material. Keeping them somewhere only I can access is far safer than leaving them on a dusty old USB stick.

As for humanoid robots in five years — I welcome them to vacuum my floors and fetch things for me. But when it comes to my digital life, I don’t need robots to be its custodian.
